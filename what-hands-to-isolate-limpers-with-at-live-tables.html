What Hands to Isolate Limpers With at Live Tables

Your iso-raise range should change based on who limped — here is how to build the right preflop range for every live cash opponent type.

By RangeIQ Poker · September 2026 · 5 min read

The question isn't whether to isolate limpers — it's which hands actually justify it. Iso-raising limpers at live cash games is one of the highest-leverage preflop plays you can make, but the correct isolation range changes based on who limped and how many players are still to act. Use the wrong hands and you're either leaving value on the table or building pots with hands that can't realize their equity.

Why Your Iso Range Depends on Fold Equity

An isolation raise has two jobs: build a pot against a weak player, and get heads-up so your hand can realize its equity. To do both, you need raw equity (your cards win at showdown) and fold equity (players behind you fold enough to keep the pot small).

At a $1/$3 table where the limper is a Calling Station or Loose Passive player, fold equity against the limper is near zero — they are calling your raise with a very wide range. That changes your iso range significantly. You want hands that perform well in a called, heads-up pot: strong pairs, broadway holdings, and suited aces with real kicker value. Small suited connectors and low suited aces lose most of their value here because they depend on the limper folding or making a big mistake with second-best hands — and a Calling Station does neither.

When the limper is a Nit or TAG, the calculus shifts. These players limp a narrow range — small pairs looking for a set, suited connectors hoping to see a cheap flop — and they will fold to a larger raise. Your fold equity against them is real, which means you can include more speculative hands in your iso range.

Building the Right Range for Each Opponent Type

Against a Calling Station or Loose Passive limper, your core iso range at $1/$3 should be: AA, KK, QQ, JJ, TT, AKs, AKo, AQs, AQo, AJs, KQs, 99, 88, A9s and above. Size to $15–$18 (5–6x) to deny multi-way action. Remove T9s, small suited connectors, and low suited aces entirely — these hands need a fold that won't come.

A9s is ahead of the Calling Station's limping range — it dominates Ax hands with weaker kickers and has clean equity vs. pocket pairs below TT. The suited nut potential adds implied odds on the right flop texture. Sizing to $15 (5x) folds the blinds and keeps the pot heads-up. At $9 (3x), the big blind calls profitably and you lose the equity edge the isolation was designed to create.

Against a Nit, shrink the iso range at the top and add speculative hands at the bottom. A Nit's limping range is often 22–66 and suited connectors — you have strong equity over that range with any pair or broadways, and fold equity means hands like 87s or T9s can be profitably added. Size smaller ($10–$12, or 4x) because a Nit will fold to any raise that looks too big relative to the pot.

Against a LAG or Recreational player who limps infrequently, treat the limp as a potential trap. These opponent types limp AA/KK more than a Nit does, so widen your iso range cautiously — premium pairs and AK remain the core, but be skeptical of slowplayed monsters when the LAG limps under the gun.

Key takeaway: Iso-raise when you have equity that beats the limper's calling range AND when your sizing can control the pot size — not because you're "supposed to" with a suited hand.

Study Your Iso Spots Between Sessions

The most common iso-raise mistake at live $1/$3 is applying a single static range to every limper, regardless of opponent type. A $15 iso with A7s is correct against a Nit; it bleeds chips against a Calling Station who will call and then lead any ace-high flop.
