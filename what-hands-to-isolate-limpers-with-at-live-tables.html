What Hands to Isolate Limpers With at Live Tables

Your iso-raise range is not fixed — it shifts with the opponent types sitting behind those limpers.

By RangeIQ Poker · August 2026 · 5 min read

Which hands should you iso-raise with when a player limps in front of you at a live $1/$3 or $2/$5 table? Most regulars get this wrong in one of two directions: they either iso too tight and surrender EV by playing passively against weak players, or they iso too wide and bleed chips when the limper turns out to be trapping or the players behind squeeze.

The isolate limpers spot is one of the most repeatable profit opportunities in live cash, but your range can't be static. The right hands depend on who limped, how many players are behind you, and where you're sitting.

Why the Iso-Raise Is a Core Profit Spot

When a Loose Passive or Calling Station limps in early or middle position, they're announcing a weak, often speculative hand — suited connectors, small pairs, or any two cards they find playable. That player has already shown they're not raising, which means they don't have a premium hand they're slowplaying at a high frequency. The iso-raise exploits this directly.

The EV comes from two sources. First, fold equity preflop: limpers fold to raises far more often than aggressive players do, especially at $1/$3 where many Recreationals and Loose Passives will muck to a $15 raise after putting in $3. Second, positional advantage postflop: when the iso works and you get heads up from the button or cutoff, you hold position on every street against a player who has already telegraphed weakness.

Isolation is not a bluff. It's a value play on your range versus their range, amplified by position. That's why the hands you choose matter — you want hands that play well heads up in position, not just any hand that beats their limping range.

The Core Range and How Opponent Type Changes It

In position (cutoff through button) against a single limper, a solid default iso range includes: 77+, AJs+, AQo+, KQs, KJs, QJs, JTs, T9s. These hands have strong equity against typical limping ranges, play well heads up, and generate enough fold equity to make the raise profitable even when called.

Against a Loose Passive or Calling Station, widen modestly: add A2s–A5s, 55–66, and T8s. These hands gain value because the Calling Station will pay you off when you make a strong hand, and their calling frequency justifies playing more speculative holdings for implied odds. Critically, do not add weak broadways like KTo or Q9o — these have poor postflop playability when called by a station who will continue with any pair.

Against a Nit, you can iso almost any hand in your standard range freely — Nits fold to iso-raises at a very high rate. If a Nit limps, they're often holding a hand they're unsure how to play preflop, not a monster. Against them, sizing matters less than against a Calling Station; even a $12 raise takes the pot often enough to be profitable.

Sizing Your Iso-Raise to Match the Table

At live $1/$3, the standard iso-raise is 3x–4x the big blind plus one big blind per limper. With one limper: raise to $12–$15. With two limpers: raise to $15–$18. The larger sizing against multiple limpers is necessary to preserve the heads-up dynamic — if your raise is too small, you get a four-way pot where your positional edge shrinks and your hand loses equity against combined ranges.

Against a Calling Station, size up within reason: $15–$18 instead of $12. They call regardless, but larger sizing extracts more from your strong hands and puts more money in the pot when you're the favorite. Do not size up so large that you fold out weak hands you want to play against — $18 is near the ceiling at $1/$3.

Key takeaway: Your iso-raise size should be large enough to discourage multiway action, not large enough to only get action from hands that beat you.

At $2/$5, bump the formula: one limper warrants $20–$25, two limpers $25–$30. The principles are identical — the numbers scale with the blind level, not the concept.
