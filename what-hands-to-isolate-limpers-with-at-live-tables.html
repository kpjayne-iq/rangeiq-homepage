What Hands to Isolate Limpers With at Live Tables

Knowing when to isolate — and with what hands — is one of the highest-EV adjustments you can make in a live cash game.

By RangeIQ Poker · August 2026 · 5 min read

When a player limps in at a live $1/$3 table, they hand you an opportunity to take control of the pot before the flop. Isolating that limper — raising to push out everyone behind you and play heads-up — is one of the most frequent and reliable exploits in live cash. But which hands are worth iso-raising, and how does that range change based on who limped? Those two questions are what this article answers.

Why Isolation Raises Work — and When They Backfire

Most live limpers have a capped range. They limp pairs they are not comfortable raising, suited connectors they will not fold, and Broadway hands they are unsure about. When you iso-raise, you accomplish three things simultaneously: you narrow the field, you put dead money in the pot from the blinds and the limper's call, and you position yourself as the aggressor on every street.

Isolation fails when the wrong conditions stack up: multiple players left to act behind you who play back aggressively, a limper who never folds, or a stack depth that makes the resulting pot awkward for your hand. At $1/$3, a standard iso-raise is 4-5x the big blind plus one big blind per limper already in. Against a single limper, that is $15-$18 from late position. Against two limpers, $18-$21.

From early position, your iso range should tighten because you have three or more players left to act and you will be playing the pot out of position. From the cutoff or button, your range opens significantly because position is locked in for every street.

Building Your Iso Range by Opponent Type

Against a Calling Station: tighten your range to hands that make strong pairs and are comfortable value-betting three streets. JJ+, AQs, AQo, AJs, KQs are clear iso-raises. Suited connectors lose value here because your equity advantage over a Station comes from made hands, not semi-bluffs that need fold equity.

Against a Recreational player (the typical "fun player" at $1/$3): widen from the cutoff and button. Recreational players call the iso-raise, then misplay medium-strength hands post-flop. Add ATs, KQo, QJs, 99, TT, and suited Aces like A9s. Their wide calling range means your top pairs gain value, and their post-flop passivity means you dictate bet sizing on every street.

Against a Nit who limped: be cautious. A Nit who limps from early position often has a hand they plan to limp-reraise — AA, KK, or occasionally AKs. From the button against a Nit UTG limp, 88-TT, AJs, and KQs are reasonable iso-raises if the Nit has a trackable flat-call tendency. K9o or A8o are not. If you iso-raise and the Nit three-bets, fold without guilt.

Against a Loose Passive: build the pot with hands that have strong top-pair or better equity. Loose Passives call down wide and rarely raise, so you want hands you can value-bet two or three streets. Your iso range looks similar to the Calling Station range: TT+, AJs+, KQs, AQo+. Avoid complex semi-bluff lines; they call too often for draws to be the plan.

Stack Depth Changes Your Range

Effective stack depth is the variable most players ignore when building their iso range. With effective stacks under 50 BBs (∼$150 at $1/$3), speculative hands like 6♣7♣ or 5♥5♠ lose value. Post-flop decisions become constrained — you are often committed to stacks by the turn, and implied odds require room to maneuver.

Deep stacks (150BB+) open the range wider. Suited connectors and small pairs gain implied odds when you can play for stacks without committing preflop. A 7-5 suited that flops a flush draw against a Recreational player's two pair is a profitable spot only if there is room for the money to go in across multiple streets.

The sweet spot for most live $1/$3 isolation plays is effective stacks between $100-$300. At this depth, ATs, KQs, 99, TT are clear iso-raises from the button against any opponent type. The gray zone — A9o, QJs, 77, 66 — depends almost entirely on the limper's opponent type and how many players remain to act behind you.
